Chapter 1 of 18

Chapter 1 — What Is a Virtue?

"When you lost your divine virtue, you will not be able to attract blessings. You will not be able to attract opportunities."

Teaching

We begin, as we must always begin, with praise. Before we ask a single question about virtue, before we define a single term, we lift our eyes and bless the Lord. My soul, and all that is within me, bless His holy name. This is not a formality. It is the doorway through which every true teaching must pass, because a heart that has not first blessed the Lord is not yet ready to receive from Him. So before we go one step further into this teaching, let us read together the words of David in Psalm 103, because everything we will discover about virtue in this book flows out of a heart postured like David's heart: grateful, worshipful, and fully awake to what God has already done.

Now to the question the Holy Spirit placed on my heart that morning: what is virtue? It is a word we use loosely in everyday speech, virtuous woman, virtuous man, as though it only describes good behaviour or moral uprightness. But when the Bible uses this word in connection with Jesus, it means something far more specific and far more powerful. Virtue, in the biblical sense we are examining, is divine ability. It is favour. It is glory. It is an aura, a covering, an anointing that flows out from a yielded life and accomplishes what natural strength alone cannot accomplish. It is the very thing that identified who Jesus was wherever He went. Apart from walking in the favour of God and the glory of God, Jesus carried a virtue, and that virtue is what enabled Him to heal the sick, cast out devils, and perform miracles. It was not merely divine power in some abstract, theological sense. It was power that could be perceived, that could be felt leaving Him, that could be received by someone who reached out in faith and touched Him.

This is why the story of the woman with the issue of blood is the central case study of this entire book. In the crowded streets of a Judean town, surrounded on every side by people pressing in on Jesus, one woman reached through the crowd and touched the hem of His garment. Immediately, Scripture tells us, her issue of blood stopped. But notice what happened on Jesus' side of that exchange. He did not simply observe a miracle happening near Him. He felt something leave His own body. "Jesus, immediately knowing in himself that virtue had gone out of him, turned him about in the press, and said, Who touched my clothes?" That is Mark's account. Luke's account uses almost identical language: "Jesus said, Somebody hath touched me: for I perceive that virtue is gone out of me." This is not poetic language. This is a description of a real spiritual transaction. Something tangible, something measurable in the spirit realm, moved from the Anointed One to the woman who reached for Him in faith.

I want you to sit with that for a moment, because it changes how we think about the Christian life. We often talk about faith as though it only affects our own inner state, our own peace of mind. But faith, when it reaches toward Jesus, actually draws something out of Him and deposits it in us. That something is virtue. It is a divine ability that heals, delivers, restores, and repositions a life. It is what the Bible calls the blessing of the Lord that maketh rich and addeth no sorrow. When virtue rests on you, whatever you touch, whatever you sell, whatever you do prospers, not because you worked harder than everyone around you, but because there is a covering, a favour, an unseen hand making a way where there was no way.

Every one of us has, at some point, either possessed a measure of this virtue or watched someone else walk in it. You have seen the person who walks into a room and doors simply open for them. You have seen the believer who prays a simple prayer and heaven answers immediately, while another person prays for years for the same thing and struggles. That difference, more often than we admit, is a matter of virtue. It is that kind of power, that kind of ability, that helps a person penetrate circumstances that would otherwise be impossible to move. It is the reason the Bible can describe some people as having favour with God and man while others, equally sincere, seem to labour without breakthrough.

But here is the sober truth this teaching must also carry: a great many people have lost their virtue. Life has become difficult for them not because God stopped loving them, but because something that once flowed freely from their lives has been interrupted, exchanged, or stolen. We will spend the whole of the next chapter examining how virtue is lost, but even here, in this foundational chapter, we must acknowledge that virtue can depart from a person the same way it left Jesus that day in the crowd, except in Jesus' case the departure was for a divine and glorious purpose, to heal a suffering woman, and in our case, more often, the departure has been an assault, a theft, an unlawful exchange. If it had been for the wrong purpose, even Jesus would have been weakened by that release of virtue, but because it served a genuine and righteous purpose, God the Father restored Him, and the Holy Ghost strengthened Him immediately afterward for the rest of His earthly ministry. This tells us something remarkable: God is willing and able to restore virtue that has departed for a right reason, and He is even more willing to restore virtue that was stolen from us wrongfully.

Understand also that virtue is not evenly distributed by accident. Scripture shows us that virtue rests differently on different people because of different callings, different levels of consecration, and different measures of grace. Esther is a striking example. It would be easy to say that God simply and arbitrarily favoured Esther above every other woman in the kingdom, but a closer reading shows us that Esther carried a certain quality of character, a certain virtue, that moved the heart of the king the moment he saw her. That virtue was not manufactured by human effort alone. It was a grace that rested upon her and distinguished her from thousands of other candidates. This is what the Bible calls being a virtuous woman, or in the case of a man, a man of honour, of substance, of weight in the spirit. It is a hallmark, a mark that sets a person apart the way gold is distinguished from ordinary metal.

I want to be careful here to correct a common misunderstanding. Virtue, in this biblical sense, is not a form of impersonal cosmic energy that anyone can tap into through technique or positive thinking. It is a distinctly Christian, distinctly Scriptural reality, rooted entirely in relationship with God and covenant with Jesus Christ. The favour that rested on Esther, the power that flowed from Jesus, the anointing that rests on a surrendered believer today, all of it flows from the same source: the Holy Spirit of God working through a life yielded to Him. There is no shortcut to virtue that bypasses righteousness, obedience, and relationship with the Lord. This is precisely why the rest of this book will walk you through repentance, forgiveness, praise, thanksgiving, and deliverance before it ever arrives at declarations of restoration. Virtue is not a technique. It is a fruit of covenant.

So as we open this book together, I want you to ask the Holy Spirit a simple, honest question: what virtue has left me? Has it been my confidence to believe God for the impossible? Has it been my favour at work, in ministry, in my family? Has it been my health, my joy, my peace? Whatever it is, hold that question gently in your heart as we move forward, because by the time you finish this book, you will not only understand what virtue is, you will know exactly how to call it back in the name of Jesus.

Before we look at the Scriptures that anchor this chapter, let us return one more time to Psalm 103, because it sets the tone for the entire journey ahead of us. David does not begin by listing his problems. He begins by remembering what God has already done: forgiven all his iniquities, healed all his diseases, redeemed his life from destruction, crowned him with lovingkindness and tender mercies, satisfied his mouth with good things so that his youth was renewed like the eagle's. This is the posture required to recover lost virtue. We do not come to God as beggars pleading with a reluctant king. We come as children reminding ourselves, out loud, of a Father who has already proven His generosity toward us. That remembering, that deliberate, vocal praise, positions our hearts to receive everything the rest of this book has been written to help you recover.

It is also worth pausing to consider why the Gospel writers chose to preserve this detail about Jesus at all. The Gospels are not sentimental documents. Every detail included in them serves a theological purpose. Luke and Mark could easily have recorded only the outcome of this encounter, that the woman was healed, without ever mentioning what Jesus felt in His own body during the exchange. The fact that both writers, independently drawing on eyewitness testimony, chose to include the detail of virtue departing from Jesus tells us that the early church understood this detail to carry lasting doctrinal significance. It was not an incidental physiological curiosity. It was proof that something real, something substantial, moved between two people in that crowded street, and that movement is the very reality this book asks you to understand and, eventually, to experience for yourself.

We should also consider the wider biblical witness to this idea of transferable spiritual substance, because the account of the woman with the issue of blood is not an isolated case. When Elisha's dead bones touched a corpse lowered hastily into his tomb, the man was raised to life, evidence that even the resting place of a Spirit-filled servant of God carried residual virtue. When handkerchiefs and aprons that had touched the Apostle Paul's skin were carried to the sick, diseases departed from them and evil spirits went out of them. These accounts are not meant to elevate objects above the Person of Christ, a mistake we will address directly in a later chapter, but they do confirm the consistent biblical pattern: virtue, once released by the Spirit of God through a yielded life, can move, can travel, and can accomplish real, measurable change wherever faith meets it.

Chapter 2 — How Virtue Is Lost

"A lot of people's value has been exchanged through demonic attack, even through eating the food of an evil person, the food of an evil eye, through sex and even through bad company."

Teaching

If the first chapter established what virtue is, this chapter must answer the harder question: how does a believer lose it? Because virtue does not simply evaporate on its own. In almost every case the Scripture and the testimony of the church point to, virtue is lost through a transaction, a breach, a door that was opened, whether knowingly or unknowingly, that allowed something precious to be drained out of a life. We are going to walk through several of these doors in this chapter, not to produce fear, but to produce discernment, because a believer who understands how virtue leaves is a believer who can guard the door going forward and, by the grace of God, call back what has already gone.

The first and most obvious way virtue is lost is through sin and disobedience. We must never sentimentalize this. Sin is not merely a private failure between an individual and God with no other consequence. Sin interrupts covenant, and covenant is the very channel through which divine virtue flows to a believer. The prophet Samuel said it plainly to King Saul: to obey is better than sacrifice, and to hearken than the fat of rams, for rebellion is as the sin of witchcraft, and stubbornness is as iniquity and idolatry. Saul lost his kingdom, his anointing, and ultimately his life because of one act of disobedience that he tried to justify as a spiritual sacrifice. Understand this: disobedience does not merely delay blessing. It severs the very connection through which virtue is sustained. When Samuel told Saul that the Lord had rejected him from being king, that rejection was the direct and immediate consequence of a broken covenant of obedience.

Solomon gives us an equally sobering example, though his failure looked different from Saul's. Solomon did not lose his virtue through a single dramatic act of rebellion. He lost it gradually, through compromise, through relationships he should never have entered, through hearts he allowed to turn his own heart away from wholehearted devotion to the Lord. Scripture tells us that Solomon loved many strange women, and that these relationships, forbidden precisely because the Lord knew they would turn Israel's hearts after other gods, did exactly that. Solomon built high places for false gods, and the Lord was angry with him because his heart had turned. The result was catastrophic: the kingdom itself, the very glory Solomon had been given, was torn from his descendants. This is what wrong company and wrong covenant partnership can do even to a man given unprecedented wisdom and unprecedented favour by God Himself. If Solomon's virtue could be eroded through compromised relationships, we must take seriously the warning about bad friendship and bad company that runs through this entire teaching. Bad company, bad friendship, can influence you, and what you carry can be lifted off you until you become empty. This is not merely a matter of poor social judgment. It is a spiritual principle. Certain relationships are structured, whether the other person realizes it or not, to draw virtue out of you the way a drain pulls water out of a basin.

Samson's story takes this even further, because Samson's loss of virtue was so specific and so physically dramatic that it has become the classic biblical illustration of virtue departing a life. Samson's strength, his virtue, was tied to a covenant he had with God from birth, symbolized outwardly by his uncut hair. Delilah, through persistent manipulation and betrayal, discovered the secret of that covenant and violated it. Scripture describes the moment with chilling simplicity: she caused his hair to be shaved, and his strength went from him. But the verse that should stop every reader in their tracks is the one that follows: Samson woke up and said, I will go out as at other times before, and shake myself, and he did not know that the Lord had departed from him. This is perhaps the most dangerous condition a believer can be in. It is not simply losing virtue. It is losing virtue and not realizing it has left. Samson still had the muscle memory of his former strength. He still had the confidence of a man who used to be unstoppable. But the substance behind that confidence, the actual virtue, was gone, and he did not know it until the Philistines were already upon him.

How many of us are walking in Samson's condition today, still speaking the way we used to speak when virtue rested heavily on our lives, still expecting the doors to open the way they used to open, without realizing that something has already left us through a compromise we underestimated. This is why this teaching insists, again and again, on discernment: you must know when to eat and when not to eat, you must know who to associate with and who to release, because the enemy rarely announces the theft of your virtue. He prefers you to keep functioning on the memory of what you used to carry while the substance quietly drains away.

Esau's story adds another layer to our understanding, because Esau did not lose his virtue through seduction or through a slow erosion of devotion. He lost it through impatience and through undervaluing what God had entrusted to him. Esau came in from the field, famished, and in that moment of physical desire, he despised something spiritual, his birthright, in exchange for a single meal of bread and pottage of lentils. Scripture's verdict on Esau is severe and instructive: thus Esau despised his birthright. The exchange of blessing between Jacob and Esau happened precisely because Esau treated something sacred as though it were disposable, tradeable, less important than an immediate appetite. This is exactly the pattern behind what this teaching calls the exchange of virtue through gifts, through food, through sexual sin, and through unlawful transactions. Every one of these exchanges follows Esau's logic: something temporary and appetitive is allowed to override something eternal and covenantal.

This brings us to a specific and sobering warning drawn directly from Proverbs: do not eat the bread of him who has an evil eye, neither desire his dainty foods. This is not a dietary instruction in the ordinary sense. It is a spiritual warning about receiving from sources that carry an assignment against you. The teaching behind this book describes how certain gifts, certain meals, certain transactions are given with a hidden intention to exchange something spiritual from the recipient. Sometimes this happens through outright occult practice, where a person is instructed by a demonic source to give things away as a means of transferring the virtue, fortune, or glory of the giver onto someone else, or of drawing virtue away from the one who receives. This should not produce paranoia in the child of God, but it must produce discernment. Not every gift is safe to receive uncritically, especially when something in your spirit registers unease about the source or the timing of that gift.

There is also the matter of sexual sin, which this teaching repeatedly names as one of the most direct pathways through which virtue is exchanged. Physical intimacy is never merely physical in Scripture. It is a covenant act, a joining of spirits as well as bodies, and it is precisely for this reason that sexual sin carries such devastating spiritual consequences. Whatever a person carries, whatever grace or anointing or favour rests on their life, is genuinely exchanged in that act of union, for better or for worse. This is why the Bible treats sexual purity with such seriousness, not merely as a matter of behavioural morality, but as a matter of spiritual stewardship over what God has deposited in a life.

Envy and unchecked bitterness round out this survey of how virtue is lost. Envy positions a person to covet what belongs to someone else rather than to steward what God has already given them, and bitterness, left unaddressed, becomes a root that defiles many, as the writer of Hebrews later warns. A heart that harbours bitterness toward another person is a heart with a door left open, and the enemy is skilled at using that open door to drain virtue from a life over time, quietly, the way water finds every crack in a foundation.

I want to close this chapter with hope rather than fear, because the entire purpose of naming these doors is to help you close them, not to leave you afraid of every relationship, every meal, and every gift for the rest of your life. The same God who allows us to see how virtue is lost has already made a way to restore everything the enemy has stolen through any of these doors. Every chapter that follows in this book will walk you toward that restoration. But we could not arrive honestly at restoration without first naming, clearly and biblically, exactly how the theft happens. Now that you have named it, you are already in a stronger position than you were at the start of this chapter, because discernment itself is a form of virtue returning to your life.

There is one more door we must name before closing this chapter, and it is perhaps the most subtle of all: the door of unexamined agreement. Many believers never consciously choose sin, wrong company, or sexual compromise, and yet still find their virtue eroding through years of small, unexamined agreements with fear, with comparison, with a scarcity mindset that quietly convinces them God's blessing is reserved for other people rather than for them. Scripture calls the enemy the accuser of the brethren, and one of his most effective strategies is not dramatic temptation but persistent, low-grade accusation, whispered so consistently over months and years that a believer begins to agree with it without ever noticing the agreement was made. This is why this teaching insists on the discipline of daily confession and daily declaration outlined in the chapters ahead. Virtue is not only lost through dramatic acts. It can also be lost through a slow, quiet surrender to doubt.

We must also address the specific danger of receiving gifts, meals, or favours from sources whose spiritual condition is unknown to us, because this pattern recurs so often in the testimonies this ministry has heard over the years. Naaman the Syrian general offers an instructive contrast to the warnings already given in this chapter. When Naaman was healed of his leprosy through the ministry of the prophet Elisha, he attempted to press valuable gifts upon Elisha as payment, and Elisha refused every one of them, understanding that receiving payment for a work of God would corrupt the testimony of that miracle. Elisha's servant Gehazi, however, secretly pursued Naaman afterward and received, under false pretenses, silver and garments that Elisha had refused. The consequence was severe: the leprosy that had left Naaman transferred to Gehazi and his descendants forever. This account stands as a permanent warning about receiving, in secret and through deception, what was never rightly offered, and about the real spiritual consequences that can follow such an exchange, regardless of how harmless it may have seemed at the time.

Chapter 3 — The Woman with the Issue of Blood: A Case Study in Virtue Withdrawn and Restored

"This woman had suffered many things of many physicians and has spent all that she had, and was nothing bettered. Rather, three words: when she had heard of Jesus."

Teaching

We return now, more slowly and more deliberately, to the account that anchors this entire book, because a single reading is not enough for a story this rich. There is a woman in Luke chapter eight and Mark chapter five who is never named, whose story takes up only a handful of verses, and yet whose testimony has become one of the most instructive accounts in all of Scripture on the subject of lost and restored virtue. I want us to walk through her story slowly, verse by verse, because every detail is doing theological work.

The first detail is her duration of suffering: twelve years. This was not a brief illness or a sudden crisis. Twelve years is enough time to grow old inside a single condition, enough time to watch friendships change, finances collapse, hope erode. Twelve years is long enough that a person begins to define their whole identity around their affliction. And in her case, the nature of the affliction, a continual issue of blood, carried an additional weight under Jewish law: it rendered her ceremonially unclean. She could not fully participate in the religious and social life of her community. Every year of her twelve years of suffering was also a year of isolation. This detail matters because it tells us that whatever virtue had left this woman, it had not simply left her body. It had left her social standing, her spiritual participation, her sense of belonging among the people of God.

The second detail is the exhaustive failure of every human remedy available to her. Mark's Gospel does not soften this: she had suffered many things of many physicians, and had spent all that she had, and was nothing bettered, but rather grew worse. This is important because it eliminates any possibility that her healing, when it came, could be attributed to natural means, timing, or coincidence. Every avenue of human help had already been tried and had already failed, and worse than failing, it had made her condition deteriorate. I want to speak directly to anyone reading this who has done everything within their natural power to fix a situation, sought every kind of help available, spent every resource they had, only to watch things grow worse instead of better. This woman's story was written for you. Her breakthrough did not come because she finally found the right physician. It came because she encountered the right Person, and there is a difference between the two that no amount of human effort can substitute for.

The third detail, and perhaps the most theologically loaded phrase in the entire account, is this: when she had heard of Jesus. Not when she saw Jesus. Not when someone laid hands on her. She heard. Faith, Scripture tells us elsewhere, comes by hearing, and hearing by the word of God. Something about what she heard concerning Jesus, His reputation, His miracles, His compassion, planted a seed of faith so strong that it overrode every physical limitation her illness had placed on her. Consider what it must have cost her to move through a dense, pressing crowd in her physical state, weakened by years of continual bleeding, socially marked as unclean, risking public exposure and shame if anyone recognized her condition. Yet she pressed forward anyway. This is what real faith does. It does not wait for ideal conditions. It moves toward Jesus even when moving is costly, even when the crowd is thick, even when reaching Him requires breaking protocols that a weaker faith would have respected.

The fourth detail is the specificity of her approach. She did not ask to be prayed for from a distance. She did not request an audience with Jesus through His disciples. She reasoned within herself, if I may touch but his clothes, I shall be whole, and she acted immediately on that reasoning. She reached out and touched the hem, the border, the fringe of His outer garment. This tells us something profound about the nature of virtue: it is transferable through faith-filled contact, even minimal contact, even contact with something as seemingly incidental as the edge of a garment. It was never about the fabric. The fabric had no power in itself. It was about the faith that reached through that fabric toward the Person wearing it. This is precisely why, in our own generation, we cannot fixate on physical objects, whether oil, water, or any other point of contact, as though the object itself carries power. Whatever significance an object carries in genuine ministry is only ever a point of contact for faith directed at Jesus Christ Himself, never a substitute for Him.

The fifth detail is instantaneous and undeniable: immediately her issue of blood stanched, or as Mark records it, straightway the fountain of her blood was dried up, and she felt in her body that she was healed of that plague. Twelve years of continual bleeding ended in a single moment of contact. There was no gradual improvement, no partial recovery requiring follow-up treatment. The virtue that flowed from Jesus accomplished, instantly and completely, what twelve years of the best available medical care could not accomplish at all.

The sixth detail returns us to Jesus' own experience of this transaction. He felt the departure of virtue from His own body, distinct and identifiable enough that He stopped in the middle of a moving, jostling crowd and asked who had touched Him. His disciples' response reveals how strange this request seemed to everyone but Jesus: Master, the multitude throng thee and press thee, and sayest thou, Who touched me? Many people were physically touching Jesus in that crush of bodies, but only one touch drew virtue out of Him, because only one touch was accompanied by faith. This distinguishes casual contact with the things of God from faith-filled contact with the Person of God. You can be near Jesus, surrounded by the things of God, even physically present in a service, without ever actually touching Him in the way that draws virtue out of heaven and into your circumstance. Proximity is not the same as faith.

The seventh detail is the woman's response once she realized she could not remain hidden. She came trembling and fell down before Jesus in the presence of everyone, and declared the whole truth, why she had touched Him and how she had been instantly healed. This public testimony matters. It would have been simpler, and far less exposing, for her to melt back into the crowd once her bleeding stopped and say nothing to anyone. Instead, she testified publicly. There is something about restored virtue that refuses to stay silent. It wants to be declared, not hidden, because a testimony spoken aloud becomes a weapon against every future attempt of the enemy to convince this woman, or anyone who hears her story, that her healing was not real.

The eighth and final detail is the tender, personal word Jesus spoke over her once she had testified: Daughter, be of good comfort: thy faith hath made thee whole; go in peace. Notice the relational language. He called her daughter, not woman, not stranger, not even by her ailment. He restored her not only physically but relationally, drawing her back into covenant family language after twelve years of social and religious isolation. And He named the mechanism of her healing precisely: thy faith hath made thee whole. Not the crowd. Not the garment. Not luck or timing. Her faith, reaching toward Jesus, is what accessed the virtue that restored her.

I believe this account was preserved in two separate Gospels, told with such careful and consistent detail, precisely so that every generation of believers who feels like this woman, drained, exhausted, having tried everything, watching things grow worse instead of better, would know exactly what to do. Hear about Jesus. Press through whatever crowd of discouragement, delay, or shame stands between you and Him. Reach out in faith, however small that reach feels. And receive what only He can give: instant, complete, undeniable restoration of what was taken from you.

This is why this chapter sits at the very centre of this book. Every principle we will study about praise, thanksgiving, confession, forgiveness, the Holy Spirit, breaking yokes, recalling stolen virtue, and healing, all of it is simply an unpacking of what this one woman did in a few desperate, faith-filled moments two thousand years ago. She is not merely a historical figure. She is a pattern, a template, for every believer who has ever said, in their heart, something has left me, and I do not know how to call it back.

There is a final layer of this case study worth drawing out, one that connects this woman's healing to the wider community around her. Mark's Gospel tells us Jesus was on His way to the house of Jairus, a ruler of the synagogue whose twelve year old daughter lay dying, when the woman with the issue of blood, who had suffered for the same span of twelve years that Jairus's daughter had been alive, interrupted His journey. It would have been easy for Jesus to treat her interruption as a delay to a more urgent, more socially prominent need. Instead, He stopped completely, gave her His full and undivided attention, and only then continued to Jairus's house, where He raised the girl from death. This detail matters greatly for our study of restored virtue: Jesus never treats the restoration of an unnamed, socially marginal woman as less important than the healing of a synagogue ruler's daughter. Every believer, regardless of social standing, financial resource, or public recognition, receives the same quality of attention and the same measure of available virtue when they reach for Him in faith. There is no tier system in the economy of God's compassion.

It is also worth noting what this woman risked by reaching out at all. Under the ceremonial law recorded in Leviticus, a woman in her condition rendered unclean anyone or anything she touched. Had she touched Jesus in a manner that was publicly known before her healing, she could have been accused of defiling a respected rabbi in front of a large crowd, an accusation that could have brought serious social and even physical consequences upon her. Her faith, then, was not a comfortable, low-risk faith. It was a faith willing to risk public shame, and possibly worse, for the sake of reaching the One she believed could restore her. This is the kind of faith this book calls you toward, not a cautious faith that reaches for Jesus only when the cost is low, but a bold faith willing to press through crowds, protocols, and even the risk of public exposure to receive what only He can give.

Chapter 4 — Praise Is the Doorway

"Bless the LORD, O my soul, and all that is within me, bless his holy name. That has to be our confession every day."

Teaching

There is a reason this teaching, and every teaching that flows from Dominion Hour with Prophet Prince, begins with praise before it moves to petition. It is not a matter of order for order's sake. It reflects a spiritual law that runs throughout Scripture: God inhabits, dwells in, is enthroned upon, the praises of His people. Psalm 22:3 states it with breathtaking directness: But thou art holy, O thou that inhabitest the praises of Israel. God does not merely receive praise the way a monarch receives applause from a distant balcony. He inhabits it. He makes His dwelling in the atmosphere that praise creates. This means that when a believer praises God authentically, they are not simply expressing gratitude, they are constructing, in the spirit realm, a throne room where God's manifest presence becomes accessible in a way it is not accessible in an atmosphere of complaint, fear, or unbelief.

This is why we cannot treat praise as a warm-up act before the real business of prayer requests begins. Praise is not the opening act. Praise is the doorway itself. If a believer wants their prayers to carry weight, wants their petitions to be heard in the throne room of heaven, they must first learn to build, through praise, the very throne upon which God chooses to sit in the midst of His people. This is precisely what happened at the beginning of the original service that gave rise to this book. Before a single request was made, before a single problem was named, we lifted our voices together and declared, bless the Lord, O my soul, and all that is within me, bless His holy name. That declaration was not incidental to what followed. It was the doorway through which everything that followed became possible.

Consider what Psalm 103 does structurally. David does not open with a list of grievances or unmet needs, even though David certainly had both throughout his life. He opens by commanding his own soul: bless the Lord. This is significant because it shows us that praise is not always a spontaneous emotional response to good circumstances. Sometimes praise is a deliberate act of the will, a command a believer gives to their own inner being, forget not all his benefits. David has to remind himself, has to actively recall, what God has already done, because the human heart left to itself will drift toward complaint, toward focusing on what is lacking rather than what has already been given. Praise, then, is partly an act of spiritual discipline, a refusal to let the soul default to ingratitude.

And what benefits does David recall? He forgives all your iniquities. He heals all your diseases. He redeems your life from destruction. He crowns you with lovingkindness and tender mercies. He satisfies your mouth with good things so that your youth is renewed like the eagle's. Notice the comprehensiveness of this list. It covers the spiritual (forgiveness of iniquity), the physical (healing of disease), the existential (redemption from destruction), the relational and emotional (lovingkindness and tender mercies), and the practical, day-to-day provision (satisfying your mouth with good things). Praise, rightly understood, is not narrow. It touches every dimension of a person's life, because God's goodness touches every dimension of a person's life.

There is a direct connection between this chapter and everything we discussed about virtue in the first three chapters of this book. If virtue is the divine ability, favour, and glory that flows from a right relationship with God, then praise is one of the primary mechanisms by which that flow is sustained and increased. This is not a mechanical formula, as though praise were a magic incantation that automatically produces blessing regardless of the heart behind it. Rather, praise is the natural posture of a heart that is already oriented rightly toward God, and God responds to that posture by drawing near, by inhabiting the space that posture creates, and by releasing more of Himself, more of His virtue, into the atmosphere that praise has prepared.

Think again of the woman with the issue of blood. Her story does not explicitly mention praise as part of her healing encounter, but consider what she did after she was healed. She fell down before Jesus and declared, before all the people, what He had done for her. That declaration, spoken aloud in a public setting, was itself an act of praise, a refusal to let the miracle remain a private, silent transaction. Praise, in this sense, is both a doorway into God's presence before the miracle and a doorway into greater testimony after the miracle. It works on both sides of the breakthrough.

We must also address why praise is so difficult for so many believers precisely during the seasons when they need it most. It is easy to praise God when circumstances are already favourable. It is far more difficult, and far more powerful, to praise God when virtue seems to have left, when the fountain of blood has not yet dried up, when twelve years have passed with no improvement. Yet it is precisely in these seasons that praise does its most important spiritual work, because praise in the midst of hardship declares to the spirit realm that our confidence rests not in our current circumstances but in the unchanging character of God. This is warfare praise, the kind of praise that does not wait for the victory to be visible before it begins celebrating the God who has already promised victory.

There is a reason so many deliverance and healing services in Scripture and in contemporary ministry begin with extended, unhurried praise before any specific ministry takes place. It is not filler. It is the construction of an atmosphere in which the manifest presence of God, and with it the flow of divine virtue, becomes tangibly accessible to everyone gathered. When a congregation, or a single believer alone in their room, genuinely blesses the Lord with all that is within them, forgetting not His benefits, they are not simply feeling good. They are opening a door.

I want to close this chapter with a practical charge. Do not wait until your circumstances justify your praise. Praise first, and let your circumstances catch up to what your praise has already declared to be true about God. This has to be our confession every day, regardless of how the day begins, regardless of what report we receive, regardless of what the enemy whispers about what has been taken from us. Bless the Lord, O my soul. This confession, repeated daily, is one of the most important disciplines you will carry with you through the rest of this book, because every subsequent chapter, thanksgiving, confession, forgiveness, the invitation of the Holy Spirit, breaking yokes, recalling stolen virtue, healing, and sealing, all of it is built on the foundation this chapter has laid. Praise is the doorway. Walk through it every single day.

Consider also the account of Jehoshaphat, king of Judah, when he faced an overwhelming military coalition arrayed against him. Rather than leading his army into battle first, he appointed singers to go out before the army, praising the beauty of holiness, saying, praise the Lord, for his mercy endureth for ever. Scripture records that when they began to sing and to praise, the Lord set ambushments against the invading armies, and they were defeated before Judah ever needed to lift a sword. This account is instructive for our purposes because it demonstrates that praise is not merely a private, internal comfort offered to a struggling believer. Praise, offered corporately and in faith before a visible breakthrough has occurred, actively participates in securing that breakthrough. The praise came first. The victory followed. This is the same order this ministry insists upon in every gathering: praise before petition, worship before warfare, because praise itself is a form of spiritual authority exercised in advance of the answer we are seeking.

We should also address a common misunderstanding about the nature of praise that can rob believers of its full power. Praise is sometimes reduced, in popular Christian culture, to musical style or emotional intensity, as though the loudness or energy of a worship service determines its spiritual effectiveness. But the praise this teaching calls for is not primarily a matter of volume or style. It is a matter of substance, a deliberate, informed recollection of who God is and what He has done, spoken and sung with sincerity regardless of the musical setting. A quiet, whispered recitation of Psalm 103 in a hospital room can carry the same spiritual weight as a loud, exuberant chorus sung by a large congregation, because what moves heaven is not decibel level but the genuine posture of a heart that has chosen to bless the Lord regardless of circumstance.

Chapter 5 of 18

Chapter 5 — The Ministry of Thanksgiving

"Thanksgiving is not a reaction to what we already have. It is a declaration over what we are still believing God for."

Teaching

There is an important distinction the Holy Spirit made clear during the original teaching that gave rise to this book, a distinction between praise and thanksgiving that is easy to overlook but essential to understand. Praise, as we studied in the previous chapter, is our declaration of who God is, His holiness, His majesty, His unchanging character. Thanksgiving is our declaration of gratitude for what God has done and what God is doing, even when what God is doing has not yet fully appeared in the visible realm. The two work together, but they are not identical, and Scripture treats the ministry of thanksgiving as its own distinct discipline with its own distinct power.

Psalm 100 gives us the clearest instruction in all of Scripture on how thanksgiving functions as an entry point into God's presence. Make a joyful noise unto the Lord, all ye lands. Serve the Lord with gladness. Come before his presence with singing. Know that the Lord he is God. And then the psalm gives us the specific instruction that anchors this entire chapter: enter into his gates with thanksgiving, and into his courts with praise. Notice the imagery. Thanksgiving is what gets you through the gate. Praise is what carries you further, into the courts themselves. This is not incidental language. It reflects the structure of the Old Testament tabernacle and temple, where a worshipper moved progressively from the outer gates toward the inner courts and ultimately into the Holy of Holies. Thanksgiving is the posture that gets you moving through that first threshold, into the presence of God at all.

This is why thanksgiving cannot be reserved only for moments when everything in our lives has already turned out well. If thanksgiving is the gate through which we enter God's presence, and we withhold thanksgiving until our circumstances justify it, we are effectively locking ourselves outside the gate during precisely the seasons when we most need to be inside. This is the deeper meaning behind Paul's instruction in 1 Thessalonians: Rejoice evermore. Pray without ceasing. In every thing give thanks: for this is the will of God in Christ Jesus concerning you. Paul does not say give thanks for every good thing that happens to you. He says give thanks in every thing, in every circumstance, in every season, whether the season is one of harvest or one of famine, whether virtue is flowing freely in your life or whether you feel, like the woman with the issue of blood, that everything has been drained from you.

I want to be careful here, because this teaching is not suggesting that a believer should manufacture false happiness about genuinely painful circumstances or pretend that hardship does not hurt. Thanksgiving, biblically understood, is not denial. It is a declaration of trust that God remains good, remains faithful, and remains at work even in circumstances that have not yet resolved. When Paul writes in every thing give thanks, he is describing a settled confidence in the character of God that does not depend on the immediate appearance of our circumstances. This is precisely the kind of thanksgiving that positions a believer to receive restored virtue, because it refuses to let present lack define the boundaries of future expectation.

There is also a corporate, ministry dimension to thanksgiving that this teaching emphasizes, drawn from the practice of beginning every gathering, whether in person or through platforms like Clubhouse, with deliberate expressions of gratitude before moving into deeper teaching or ministry. This is not merely a stylistic choice. It reflects the conviction that a gathered body of believers who take time to genuinely thank God together create a spiritual atmosphere entirely different from a gathering that moves immediately into requests, complaints, or even urgent intercession without first establishing gratitude as the foundation. Thanksgiving, offered corporately, has a way of unifying a gathered body around the goodness of God rather than around a shared list of grievances, however legitimate those grievances might be.

Consider the far-reaching implications of thanksgiving in the life of Job, whose story we will return to more fully in a later chapter on restoration. Before his restoration came, before the Lord turned his captivity and gave him twice as much as he had before, Job worshipped in the middle of catastrophic loss, declaring, the Lord gave, and the Lord hath taken away; blessed be the name of the Lord. This is thanksgiving offered before restoration, not after it, and Scripture is careful to record that it was only after Job prayed for his friends, in a posture of continued faith and worship despite unresolved loss, that his captivity was turned and his fortunes doubled. There is a pattern here worth naming plainly: thanksgiving offered in the midst of loss often precedes, and in some sense prepares the ground for, the very restoration a believer is hoping to see.

This chapter, then, is not asking you to wait until your virtue has been fully restored before you begin thanking God. It is asking you to begin thanking God now, in whatever condition you currently find yourself, as an act of faith that positions you to walk through the gates into His presence, where restoration is found. Make a joyful noise, the psalmist says, not because every circumstance in your life has resolved, but because the Lord is good, His mercy is everlasting, and His truth endures to all generations regardless of what your current season looks like.

I want to offer a practical exercise as we close this chapter. Take a moment right now, before moving to the next chapter, and name aloud, specifically, three things you are thankful for today, not generic things, but specific evidences of God's goodness in your actual life circumstances. Then take a moment to thank Him, in advance, for the restoration this book is helping you pursue, even though you may not yet see it manifested. This is the ministry of thanksgiving in its fullest sense: gratitude for the past, gratitude for the present, and gratitude offered in faith for what is not yet seen but is already promised.

Rejoice evermore. Pray without ceasing. In every thing give thanks. This has to become, alongside bless the Lord, O my soul from our previous chapter, a daily rhythm woven into the fabric of your ordinary life, not reserved for Sunday services or special occasions, but practiced at your kitchen table, in your car, at your workplace, in the quiet moments before sleep. Thanksgiving offered continually is one of the most underestimated weapons available to the believer pursuing the restoration of everything the enemy has stolen.

The apostle Paul models this ministry of thanksgiving throughout his letters, even though many of those letters were written from difficult circumstances, including imprisonment. Writing to the Philippians from a Roman prison, Paul instructs believers, be careful for nothing, but in every thing by prayer and supplication with thanksgiving let your requests be made known unto God. Notice that Paul does not separate thanksgiving from the act of making requests. He weaves them together, teaching us that the healthiest kind of petition is one soaked in gratitude even before the answer arrives. This is not a technique for manipulating God into responding favourably. It is a description of a heart posture that trusts God's character enough to thank Him in the very same breath it is asking Him for help.

We should also consider the corrosive opposite of thanksgiving: murmuring and complaint, which Scripture treats with far greater seriousness than casual conversation would suggest. The children of Israel, delivered miraculously from Egypt through the parting of the Red Sea, quickly fell into a pattern of murmuring against Moses and against God whenever their circumstances became difficult in the wilderness. Scripture records that this pattern of complaint, rather than thanksgiving, directly contributed to an entire generation failing to enter the promised land. Their unbelief was not merely a private attitude problem. It shaped the trajectory of their entire future. This sobering account should motivate us to examine our own patterns of speech honestly. A believer who has developed a habit of complaint, even about legitimately difficult circumstances, is cultivating an atmosphere entirely opposite to the one this chapter describes, an atmosphere that can delay rather than accelerate the very restoration they are hoping to see.

Chapter 6 of 18

Chapter 6 — The Blood That Cleanses: Confession and Mercy

"Have mercy upon me, O God, according to thy lovingkindness, according unto the multitude of thy tender mercies blot out my transgressions. That has to be our confession too, before we go further."

Teaching

Every teaching we have covered so far, virtue, its loss, its recovery in the woman with the issue of blood, praise, and thanksgiving, has been leading us toward this necessary and sobering chapter, because no believer can honestly pursue restored virtue while ignoring the reality of sin that stands between them and God. This is precisely why, in the original service that produced this book, after praise and thanksgiving had established the atmosphere, the very next movement was confession, led directly through the words of Psalm 51, David's prayer after his sin with Bathsheba and Uriah was exposed by the prophet Nathan.

Psalm 51 is one of the most raw, unguarded prayers in all of Scripture, and its rawness is precisely why it belongs at the centre of any honest pursuit of restoration. David does not minimize his sin. He does not offer excuses about the pressures of kingship or the temptations of power. He simply pleads for mercy on the basis of God's character, not his own: have mercy upon me, O God, according to thy lovingkindness, according unto the multitude of thy tender mercies blot out my transgressions. Notice that David's appeal rests entirely on who God is, His lovingkindness, His tender mercies, not on any argument about what David deserves. This is the posture every believer must adopt when approaching God for forgiveness. We do not come demonstrating why we deserve mercy. We come appealing entirely to the character of the One who delights in showing mercy.

David continues with specific, unflinching acknowledgment: wash me throughly from mine iniquity, and cleanse me from my sin, for I acknowledge my transgressions, and my sin is ever before me. Against thee, thee only, have I sinned, and done this evil in thy sight. This is genuine confession, not a vague acknowledgment that mistakes were made, but a specific, personal ownership of wrongdoing before God. David then moves into a theological reflection that has troubled and comforted believers for three thousand years: behold, I was shapen in iniquity, and in sin did my mother conceive me. This is not David blaming his mother or excusing his own choices by appeal to inherited sinfulness. It is David acknowledging the depth of humanity's fallen condition, a condition that makes the mercy he is asking for even more necessary and even more remarkable when it is granted.

From this place of honest acknowledgment, David asks for something specific: purge me with hyssop, and I shall be clean, wash me, and I shall be whiter than snow. Hyssop was used in the Old Testament in purification rituals, including the original Passover, when hyssop was used to apply the blood of the lamb to the doorposts of Israelite homes in Egypt. David is invoking the entire sacrificial system, pointing forward, whether he fully understood it or not, to the ultimate cleansing that would come through the blood of a far greater sacrifice than any lamb slain under the old covenant. Make me to hear joy and gladness, David continues, that the bones which thou hast broken may rejoice. Hide thy face from my sins, and blot out all mine iniquities. Create in me a clean heart, O God, and renew a right spirit within me. Cast me not away from thy presence, and take not thy holy spirit from me. Restore unto me the joy of thy salvation, and uphold me with thy free spirit.

This last line deserves particular attention in a book about restoring lost virtue, because David is explicitly asking God to restore the joy of his salvation, language that echoes precisely what this entire book has been describing. Sin had drained something from David, not his salvation itself, which remained secure, but the joy, the experiential reality, the felt presence of God's favour in his daily life. This is exactly the kind of loss we have been examining throughout this book, and Psalm 51 shows us the precise pathway back: honest confession, appeal to God's mercy rather than our own merit, and a specific request for restoration, not merely forgiveness in the abstract, but the concrete restoration of joy, of a clean heart, of the right spirit, and of the sustaining presence of the Holy Spirit.

We must place this alongside the New Testament's clearest teaching on confession and cleansing, found in the first letter of John. If we walk in the light, as he is in the light, we have fellowship one with another, and the blood of Jesus Christ his Son cleanseth us from all sin. If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves, and the truth is not in us. If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins, and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness. This passage removes any excuse for a believer to pretend sinlessness, and it removes any excuse for a believer to remain in unconfessed sin, because the promise attached to confession is breathtakingly generous: God is not merely willing but faithful and just to forgive, and not merely to forgive, but to cleanse from all unrighteousness. Notice that this cleansing is tied directly to the blood of Jesus Christ, the same blood David's confession in Psalm 51 was pointing toward centuries before Calvary.

This is also where we must return to the language of the blood in Revelation, because this teaching consistently emphasizes the finished work of Jesus Christ, sealed and validated through His blood, as the only legitimate basis for cleansing, deliverance, and restoration. Unto him that loved us, and washed us from our sins in his own blood, and hath made us kings and priests unto God and his Father. This is not a symbolic or metaphorical washing. It is the actual spiritual mechanism by which sin, which severs us from the flow of divine virtue, is dealt with permanently and completely. Every act of confession we make is not merely an emotional release. It is an appeal to a legal, covenantal transaction already accomplished at the cross, where the blood of Jesus was shed precisely so that our sins could be washed away and our fellowship with God restored.

I want to speak plainly here about why this chapter cannot be skipped on the way to recovering lost virtue. It would be tempting, in a book like this, to move quickly from identifying lost virtue to declaring its restoration, skipping the uncomfortable work of confession in between. But this is precisely the shortcut that produces shallow, short-lived breakthroughs rather than lasting restoration. Sin, left unconfessed, remains an open door through which virtue continues to drain even while a believer is speaking declarations over their life. This is why the original service that produced this book placed confession, modeled directly on Psalm 51, immediately after thanksgiving and before any deliverance ministry took place. The order matters. We cannot bypass the blood that cleanses on our way to the virtue that restores. They are not two separate things. The cleansing is the doorway to the restoration.

If you sense, as you read this chapter, specific sin the Holy Spirit is bringing to your attention, do not rush past this conviction toward the more exciting chapters ahead on breaking yokes and recalling stolen virtue. Stop here. Confess specifically. Receive, by faith, the promise of 1 John 1:9, that He is faithful and just to forgive you and to cleanse you from all unrighteousness. Let the blood of Jesus do its complete work in you before you move forward, because everything this book promises to help you recover depends on this foundation being genuinely, honestly laid.

It is worth pausing here to address a pastoral concern that arises often in ministry to believers seeking restored virtue: the difference between godly conviction, which leads to confession and life, and worldly condemnation, which leads only to shame and further bondage. Paul distinguishes these clearly in his second letter to the Corinthians, writing that godly sorrow worketh repentance to salvation not to be repented of, but the sorrow of the world worketh death. Godly conviction, the kind the Holy Spirit brings, always points toward a specific action, confess this, forsake that, receive this mercy, and it always leaves the door open toward restored fellowship with God. Worldly condemnation, by contrast, is vague, relentless, and offers no path forward. It simply repeats an accusation without ever inviting genuine repentance. If you find yourself feeling perpetually condemned rather than specifically convicted, that feeling is not coming from the Holy Spirit, and it must be resisted with the same confidence David expressed when he asked God to blot out his transgressions rather than simply reminding himself of them forever.

There is also a communal dimension to confession that Scripture does not want us to overlook. James instructs believers, confess your faults one to another, and pray one for another, that ye may be healed. While the deepest confession must always be made directly to God, there is a specific kind of healing that becomes available when a believer, in appropriate and safe relationship with mature, trustworthy fellow believers, brings hidden sin into the light rather than carrying it in isolation. Secrecy is one of sin's most effective weapons, because sin kept hidden in darkness tends to fester, while sin brought honestly into a safe, godly relationship often loses much of its power. This is part of why this ministry values ongoing fellowship, discussed further in our closing chapter, rather than a purely private, individualized approach to spiritual growth.

Chapter 7 of 18

Chapter 7 — Forgiving Others to Unlock Heaven

"If we forgive men their trespasses, our heavenly Father will also forgive us. This is not optional. This is the condition."

Teaching

There is a hard, unavoidable teaching embedded in the pattern of the deliverance flow this ministry follows every time we gather, and it comes immediately after confession, not as an afterthought, but as a necessary companion to it. We cannot honestly ask God to forgive us while refusing to forgive others. Jesus made this connection explicit, not implicit, in His teaching on prayer: for if ye forgive men their trespasses, your heavenly Father will also forgive you, but if ye forgive not men their trespasses, neither will your Father forgive your trespasses. This is not a suggestion offered alongside other equally valid options. Jesus states it as a direct condition. Our forgiveness of others and our reception of forgiveness from God are bound together in a way we cannot separate, however uncomfortable that binding might feel to a heart still nursing a grievance.

Mark's Gospel records Jesus teaching this same principle in the context of prayer more broadly: and when ye stand praying, forgive, if ye have ought against any, that your Father also which is in heaven may forgive you your trespasses, but if ye do not forgive, neither will your Father which is in heaven forgive your trespasses. Notice the setting Jesus chooses for this teaching: prayer itself. He is telling us that unforgiveness does not merely damage our relationships with other people. It obstructs our communication with heaven. A believer can pray fervently, fast diligently, and declare boldly, but if unforgiveness remains lodged in their heart, that same believer has erected a barrier between themselves and the very throne room they are trying to access through their prayers.

This connects directly to everything we have studied about virtue in the earlier chapters of this book. If virtue flows from a right relationship with God, and unforgiveness obstructs that relationship at its root, then unforgiveness is one of the most significant, and most commonly overlooked, doors through which virtue is lost or through which its restoration is delayed. A believer can pursue every other principle in this book faithfully, praise, thanksgiving, confession, and yet remain empty, dry, and unrestored simply because they have not released someone who wounded them.

I want to address the difficulty of this teaching honestly, because forgiveness, when it involves real, deep, sometimes ongoing harm, is rarely simple or immediate. Forgiveness is not the same as saying that what was done to you did not matter, or that it did not hurt, or that the person who hurt you deserves to escape all consequences for their actions. Forgiveness, biblically understood, is a decision to release a person from the debt you feel they owe you, entrusting the matter of justice entirely into God's hands rather than carrying it yourself. This is precisely what allows a believer to walk in freedom regardless of whether the person who wronged them ever apologizes, ever changes, or ever faces any visible consequence at all. Your freedom, your restored virtue, cannot be made contingent on someone else's repentance, because if it were, the enemy would simply ensure that repentance never comes, and you would remain bound indefinitely.

Consider the broader picture this teaching paints of covenant relationship with God. We have been forgiven an enormous debt through the blood of Jesus Christ, a debt we could never have repaid through our own effort or merit. Jesus' own parables elsewhere illustrate this vividly: a servant forgiven an impossibly large debt by his master turns around and refuses to forgive a fellow servant a comparatively small debt, and the master's response to that servant's hypocrisy is severe. We cannot receive the immeasurable mercy of God for our own sin while withholding a comparatively smaller mercy from someone who has wronged us. This is not merely poor spiritual etiquette. It is, according to Jesus' own words, a direct barrier to receiving the forgiveness we ourselves need.

This teaching also intersects directly with the theme of restored virtue in a practical way. Bitterness, which we touched on briefly in our chapter on how virtue is lost, is simply unforgiveness that has been allowed to take root and grow. The writer of Hebrews warns that a root of bitterness, if it springs up, troubles a person and defiles many others around them. Bitterness does not remain contained within the heart that carries it. It spreads, it colours relationships, it affects tone of voice, facial expression, willingness to trust, willingness to receive good things from others. A believer walking in unforgiveness often cannot recognize restored virtue even when it arrives, because their spiritual senses have been dulled by the ongoing internal conflict that bitterness produces.

I want to offer a practical process for anyone reading this chapter who recognizes unforgiveness in their own heart. First, name specifically who you need to forgive and what specifically they did. Vague forgiveness, forgiving in general without naming the actual wound, rarely produces lasting freedom. Second, acknowledge honestly, before God, the real pain that this offence caused you. Do not minimize it in an attempt to appear more spiritually mature than you feel. Third, make a deliberate decision, an act of the will rather than merely an emotion, to release this person from the debt you have been holding against them, entrusting justice to God rather than carrying it yourself. Fourth, ask God to bless this person, even if you do not yet feel warmth toward them, because this act of blessing, spoken even through gritted teeth at first, begins to dismantle the bitterness that unforgiveness produces. Fifth, if appropriate and safe, consider whether reconciliation is possible, understanding that forgiveness does not always require restored relationship, especially in cases involving ongoing danger or abuse, but it always requires the release of the debt in your own heart.

This process is not a one-time event for most people. Some wounds are deep enough that forgiveness must be renewed repeatedly as memories resurface and old pain reasserts itself. This is not a sign of failure. It is simply the ongoing nature of forgiveness in a fallen world. What matters is the direction of your heart, continually choosing release over resentment, continually choosing to entrust justice to God rather than to your own efforts at retaliation or control.

As we move forward into the remaining chapters of this book, chapters that will teach us to invite the Holy Ghost more fully, to break yokes, to recall stolen virtue, and to walk in healing and restoration, I want you to carry this chapter's teaching with you as a foundation. Unforgiveness, left unaddressed, will quietly undermine every declaration you speak and every deliverance prayer you pray. But forgiveness extended, however difficult, however costly, opens the very heaven that this entire book is helping you access. If ye forgive men their trespasses, your heavenly Father will also forgive you. This is not optional. This is the condition, and it is a condition we can meet, by grace, through the same power that enabled Jesus, hanging on the cross, to say, Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do.

Peter once asked Jesus directly how many times he was required to forgive a brother who sinned against him, suggesting seven times as a generous upper limit. Jesus answered, I say not unto thee, until seven times, but until seventy times seven, a number meant not as a literal ceiling to be counted toward but as a way of saying forgiveness has no ceiling at all. Jesus then told the parable of the unforgiving servant to illustrate this point, a servant forgiven an enormous, unpayable debt by his master, who then refused to forgive a fellow servant a comparatively tiny debt. When the master learned of this, he delivered the unforgiving servant to the tormentors until he should pay all that was due, and Jesus closed the parable with a sobering warning: so likewise shall my heavenly Father do also unto you, if ye from your hearts forgive not every one his brother their trespasses. This parable should settle, once and for all, any question about whether forgiveness is optional for the believer pursuing restored virtue. It is not.

I want to acknowledge, with pastoral honesty, that some readers of this chapter carry wounds so severe, abuse, betrayal, abandonment, that the command to forgive may feel almost impossible to obey in your own strength. This is precisely why forgiveness, in the pattern this book has followed from its opening pages, is never asked of us in isolation from the rest of the deliverance flow. Forgiveness becomes possible, sustainable, and complete when it flows out of genuine praise, thanksgiving, confession, and the empowering presence of the Holy Ghost we will examine more fully in the next chapter. You are not asked to forgive through sheer willpower alone. You are invited to forgive as someone who has already received far greater mercy than you are being asked to extend, and who is being filled, even now, with the same Spirit who empowered Jesus to forgive from the cross itself.

Chapter 8 of 18

Chapter 8 — Inviting the Holy Ghost: Fire That Restores

"Create in me a clean heart, O God, and renew a right spirit within me. We cannot do what we are about to do without the Holy Ghost leading us."

Teaching

After praise, thanksgiving, confession, and forgiveness have prepared the ground, this ministry never moves directly into deliverance ministry or bold declaration without one more essential step: a deliberate, unhurried invitation for the Holy Spirit to move in fullness among the gathered believers. This is not a formality inserted for the sake of tradition. It reflects a conviction at the very heart of this teaching: nothing that follows, breaking yokes, recalling stolen virtue, walking in healing, sealing what has been restored, can be accomplished through human effort, technique, or willpower alone. It requires the tangible, manifest presence and power of the Holy Ghost.

David's prayer in Psalm 51 gives us the language for this invitation. Create in me a clean heart, O God, and renew a right spirit within me. Cast me not away from thy presence, and take not thy holy spirit from me. Then, continuing into the verse we did not fully examine in our earlier chapter on confession, David says, restore unto me the joy of thy salvation, and uphold me with thy free spirit. Then will I teach transgressors thy ways, and sinners shall be converted unto thee. David understood something essential: the restoration he sought was not merely a private, internal experience. It was meant to overflow into ministry to others, and that overflow could only happen through the sustaining, empowering presence of God's Spirit remaining active in his life.

This connects directly to the ministry of the Holy Ghost as revealed at Pentecost, the definitive moment when the promised Spirit was poured out upon the gathered believers in Jerusalem. And when the day of Pentecost was fully come, they were all with one accord in one place. And suddenly there came a sound from heaven as of a rushing mighty wind, and it filled all the house where they were sitting. And there appeared unto them cloven tongues like as of fire, and it sat upon each of them. And they were all filled with the Holy Ghost, and began to speak with other tongues, as the Spirit gave them utterance. Notice the details of this account: unity, one accord, one place, preceded this outpouring. The wind and fire were not subtle or easily missed. Every person present was filled, not merely a select few. And the immediate result was empowered, Spirit-led utterance that transcended the natural abilities of those gathered.

I want to draw a direct line between Pentecost and the restoration of virtue we have been examining throughout this book. The virtue that flowed from Jesus during His earthly ministry, the same virtue that healed the woman with the issue of blood, did not disappear when Jesus ascended to heaven. Instead, He promised, and delivered, an even greater outpouring of that same divine power through the Holy Spirit poured out on all believers. This is why an authentic, sustained invitation for the Holy Ghost to move is not a peripheral element of this teaching but central to it. If virtue flows through the Spirit of God working in and through a yielded life, then a life that is not consciously, deliberately inviting and yielding to the Holy Spirit's presence is a life that has, in effect, closed one of the primary channels through which virtue is meant to flow.

Revelation gives us another image of the Holy Spirit's fullness that this teaching draws on directly: and out of the throne proceeded lightnings and thunderings and voices, and there were seven lamps of fire burning before the throne, which are the seven Spirits of God. This imagery of the sevenfold Spirit of God, drawn also from Isaiah's prophecy of the Spirit that would rest upon the Messiah, the spirit of the Lord, the spirit of wisdom and understanding, the spirit of counsel and might, the spirit of knowledge and of the fear of the Lord, points to the comprehensive, multifaceted nature of the Holy Spirit's ministry in a believer's life. He is not merely a single, generic spiritual influence. He is wisdom for confused decisions, understanding for confusing circumstances, counsel for uncertain paths, might for impossible tasks, knowledge for hidden things, and reverent fear that keeps a believer anchored in humility even as they walk in increasing power.

This is why, in the original service that gave rise to this book, the invitation to the Holy Ghost included specific requests: open our understanding the way You opened the understanding of the disciples on the road to Emmaus, so we can see clearly things hidden from our natural eyes. Wide open our eyes, the way the psalmist prayed in Psalm 119, open thou mine eyes, that I may behold wondrous things out of thy law. These are not vague or generic prayers. They are specific invitations for the Holy Spirit to illuminate exactly the kind of blind spots and hidden strongholds this book has been helping us identify: the doors through which virtue was lost, the unforgiveness that has obstructed our prayers, the specific altars and agreements that need to be renounced in the chapters still ahead of us.

I want to address a common misunderstanding about the Holy Spirit's fire, since fire is such a prominent image throughout this teaching and throughout Scripture more broadly. The fire of the Holy Ghost is never described in Scripture as a wild, uncontrolled, destructive force unleashed without purpose. It is a refining, purifying, empowering fire, the same kind of fire that fell on the altar during significant moments of covenant renewal throughout Israel's history, consuming what needed to be consumed while leaving intact everything that belonged to God. When we invite the Holy Ghost's fire into our lives in the context of restoring lost virtue, we are not asking for chaos or for an experience disconnected from Scripture and sound doctrine. We are asking for the same fire that fell at Pentecost, empowering ordinary believers to walk and minister in extraordinary ways, entirely consistent with the character and Word of God.

This chapter, then, functions as a hinge point in the structure of this book. Everything before it, understanding virtue, recognizing how it is lost, studying the woman with the issue of blood, praising, giving thanks, confessing, and forgiving, has prepared the ground. Everything after it, breaking yokes, recalling stolen virtue, walking in healing, and sealing what has been restored, requires the active, ongoing empowerment of the Holy Ghost to actually accomplish. We cannot do what comes next in our own strength. We need the same Spirit that filled the house at Pentecost, the same sevenfold Spirit that burns before the throne, to fill this moment, this reading, this prayer, with His tangible presence and power.

Before you move to the next chapter, I want to invite you to pause, however you are reading this book, alone in a quiet room or gathered with others, and simply ask the Holy Spirit to come. Not with elaborate language, not with performance, but with the same simple honesty David brought to his prayer for a clean heart and a right spirit. Ask Him to fill you, to open your understanding, to wide open your eyes, and to empower you for everything the remaining chapters of this book will invite you to do.

Jesus Himself, on the night before His crucifixion, prepared His disciples for the coming of the Holy Spirit with language that directly reinforces everything this chapter teaches: nevertheless I tell you the truth, it is expedient for you that I go away, for if I go not away, the Comforter will not come unto you, but if I depart, I will send him unto you. This is a remarkable statement. Jesus tells His closest followers that His physical departure would actually benefit them, because it would make way for the Holy Spirit's ongoing, indwelling presence, no longer limited to physical proximity to Jesus in one geographic location, but available to every believer, everywhere, at all times. This is directly relevant to our study of virtue, because it means the virtue that once flowed only from physical contact with Jesus, as it did for the woman with the issue of blood, is now available continuously, internally, through the abiding presence of the Holy Spirit in every believer who has received Him.

This is also why this ministry teaches believers not to treat the Holy Spirit as a mysterious, unpredictable force to be approached with uncertainty, but as a Person to be known, welcomed, and continually invited deeper into every area of life. Paul instructs the Ephesian believers, be not drunk with wine, wherein is excess, but be filled with the Spirit, using a grammatical construction in the original language that implies continuous, repeated filling rather than a single, one-time event. This means the invitation we are practicing in this chapter is not meant to be a rare or occasional occurrence reserved only for dramatic services. It is meant to become the daily rhythm of a believer's life, waking each morning with a fresh invitation, Holy Spirit, fill me again today, lead me again today, empower me again today for whatever this day requires.

Chapter 9 of 18

Chapter 9 — Breaking Yokes and Renouncing Every Evil Altar

"By Genesis 27:40, when I have the dominion I break every yoke from off my neck in the name of Jesus. That yoke was never meant to stay."

Teaching

There comes a point in every genuine deliverance service where teaching must give way to confrontation, where understanding what has happened to us must lead directly into actively, verbally, and by faith undoing it. This chapter marks that turning point in our journey together. We have identified what virtue is, how it is lost, how it can be recovered through the pattern of the woman with the issue of blood, and we have walked through the necessary preparatory work of praise, thanksgiving, confession, forgiveness, and inviting the Holy Ghost. Now we come to the direct, authoritative work of breaking yokes and renouncing every altar that has no legal right to hold what belongs to the child of God.

The prophet Isaiah gives us the foundational promise for this entire chapter: and it shall come to pass in that day, that his burden shall be taken away from off thy shoulder, and his yoke from off thy neck, and the yoke shall be destroyed because of the anointing. Notice carefully what breaks the yoke in this verse. It is not human effort, willpower, or determination alone. It is the anointing, the very virtue we have been studying throughout this book. This tells us something crucial about the nature of yoke-breaking: it is not primarily a psychological exercise in positive thinking or self-improvement. It is a spiritual reality accomplished through the anointing of God resting upon a life, the same anointing that flowed from Jesus to heal, deliver, and restore everyone who reached for Him in faith.

The story of Jacob and Esau, which we examined earlier in this book regarding the loss of Esau's birthright, contains a companion prophecy that speaks directly to yoke-breaking. When Isaac blessed Esau after Jacob had already received the primary blessing, he spoke these words: behold, thy dwelling shall be the fatness of the earth, and of the dew of heaven from above, and by thy sword shalt thou live, and shalt serve thy brother, and it shall come to pass when thou shalt have the dominion, that thou shalt break his yoke from off thy neck. This is a remarkable prophetic pattern: even in a season of subjugation, even under a yoke that has been legitimately or illegitimately placed upon a person's life, there comes a time, appointed by God, when dominion is restored and the yoke is broken. This ministry holds fast to this pattern as a promise over every believer who has found themselves under a yoke of oppression, poverty, sickness, or generational bondage. The yoke is not permanent. When dominion comes, and dominion is exactly what the tagline of this ministry declares, breaking yokes, restoring destinies, establishing dominion, the yoke breaks.

We must also address directly the matter of satanic writings, ordinances, and legal pronouncements that this teaching consistently identifies as one of the primary mechanisms by which yokes are established and maintained over a life. Paul's letter to the Colossians gives us the theological and legal basis for dismantling these: and you, being dead in your sins and the uncircumcision of your flesh, hath he quickened together with him, having forgiven you all trespasses, blotting out the handwriting of ordinances that was against us, which was contrary to us, and took it out of the way, nailing it to his cross. This is powerful legal language, drawn from the practice of nailing a certificate of debt to a public place once that debt had been paid in full, cancelling it publicly and permanently. Whatever handwriting, whatever legal document in the spirit realm has been drawn up against your life, whether through generational sin, personal disobedience, or demonic assignment, has already been dealt with at the cross. Jesus did not merely forgive the debt privately. He took the certificate of that debt and nailed it publicly to His cross, declaring it cancelled before every principality and power watching.

This is the legal foundation behind every prayer of renunciation this ministry prays. When we renounce an evil altar, a generational covenant, or a demonic transaction, we are not engaging in superstition or manipulating unseen forces through clever wording. We are standing on a legal reality already accomplished by Christ and declaring, out loud, that this reality applies to our specific situation. Any Pharaoh that refuses to let us go must reckon with the written judgment of the just God, a judgment already rendered in our favour through the finished work of the cross.

I want to be careful and precise here, in keeping with the standards this ministry holds to consistently: breaking yokes and renouncing altars through this teaching never involves physical rituals detached from Christ. There are no charms, no objects imbued with power apart from Him, no rites performed for their own sake. Where anointing oil is used in this ministry's practice, it is always and only a point of contact for faith already directed at Jesus Christ, prayed over and applied as an act of obedience and expectancy, never as a substance carrying independent spiritual power. The yoke is broken because of the anointing of the Holy Spirit and the finished, legal work of the cross, nothing more and nothing less.

Renouncing an evil altar means specifically and verbally rejecting any agreement, covenant, or dedication, whether made knowingly by the believer or unknowingly by a previous generation, that has given the enemy legal ground to oppress an area of a person's life. This is not a matter of blaming ancestors or living in fear of generational curses as though they carry more power than the blood of Jesus. It is simply an acknowledgment that Scripture describes real consequences passing through family lines, and that the appropriate biblical response is not fear but authoritative renunciation, standing in the freedom Christ has already purchased and specifically applying that freedom to whatever door has remained open.

The prayer points this ministry has consistently used for this portion of a deliverance service are direct and unambiguous: by Colossians 2:14, every handwriting of ordinance, every satanic law and pronouncement against me is blotted out and nailed to the cross. Any Pharaoh that refuses to let me go, receive the written judgment of the just God. By Genesis 27:40, when I have the dominion I break every yoke from off my neck in the name of Jesus. Every stubborn spirit behind my situation, be tamed and finished by the fire of the Holy Ghost. These are not casual words. They are declared with the same authority Jesus gave every believer when He said, behold, I give unto you power to tread on serpents and scorpions, and over all the power of the enemy, and nothing shall by any means hurt you. This verse, Luke chapter ten and verse nineteen, stands as the anchor beneath every act of spiritual authority this ministry exercises. It is not our own authority we are exercising. It is authority given to us, delegated to us, by Jesus Christ Himself.

As you pray through the declarations at the close of this chapter, I want you to pray them with full confidence, not because the words themselves carry magical power, but because they are aligned entirely with what Christ has already accomplished and with the authority He has already given to every believer who belongs to Him. The yoke on your neck, whatever specific form it has taken in your life, is not stronger than the anointing, is not more permanent than the cross, and is not beyond the reach of the dominion Jesus has already established over every power of the enemy.

The prophet Nahum reminds us of another dimension of yoke-breaking worth naming here, the certainty of God's timing even when deliverance seems delayed. Nahum prophesied concerning the Assyrian oppression of Israel, declaring that though the Assyrian yoke had pressed heavily upon God's people for years, the Lord would break it, saying, for now will I break his yoke from off thee, and will burst thy bonds in sunder. This prophecy came after a long season of oppression, not before it, reminding us that the timing of yoke breaking belongs to God's sovereign wisdom, not to our own impatient calculation. A yoke that has remained in place for years does not indicate that God has forgotten or that deliverance is impossible. It often simply means the appointed time has not yet fully come, and our responsibility in the meantime is to remain faithful, to keep declaring truth over our situation, and to trust that the same God who broke Assyria's yoke off Israel's neck is entirely capable of breaking whatever yoke presses upon us today.

We should also address the specific matter of generational altars, since this teaching addresses them directly and they represent one of the more misunderstood aspects of deliverance ministry within the wider church. A generational altar refers to a dedication, covenant, or ongoing spiritual agreement made by ancestors, sometimes through idol worship, sometimes through occult practice, sometimes through simple persistent sin, that continues to exert spiritual influence over a family line until it is specifically renounced. Scripture acknowledges this reality without ever suggesting believers should live in fear of it. The second commandment warns that the iniquity of the fathers can visit the children to the third and fourth generation of those who hate God, but immediately balances this warning with the promise of mercy unto thousands of generations of them that love God and keep His commandments. The weight of this passage falls decisively on the side of mercy, not fear. A believer who has come to Christ, confessed sin, forgiven others, and now stands to renounce every evil altar is not engaging in a desperate struggle against overwhelming odds. They are simply applying, specifically and by name, a freedom that Christ has already secured completely at the cross.

Chapter 10 of 18

Chapter 10 — Recall and Restoration: Calling Back What Was Stolen

"I call my virtues back in the name of Jesus. I call my breakthroughs back in the name of Jesus. Whatever the enemy took from me, let it be restored."

Teaching

We arrive now at the chapter that gives this entire book its title, because everything we have studied so far, understanding virtue, recognizing its loss, praising, giving thanks, confessing, forgiving, inviting the Holy Ghost, and breaking every yoke, has been building toward this moment: the moment of active, faith-filled recall. Restoration in Scripture is rarely passive. It is not simply something that happens to a believer while they wait quietly. It is something a believer actively, verbally, and confidently calls forth, standing on the promises and the character of God.

The prophet Joel gives us one of the most beloved and frequently quoted restoration promises in all of Scripture, and it is worth reading slowly and completely rather than only quoting its most famous line. Be glad then, ye children of Zion, and rejoice in the Lord your God, for he hath given you the former rain moderately, and he will cause to come down for you the rain, the former rain, and the latter rain in the first month. And the floors shall be full of wheat, and the vats shall overflow with wine and oil. And I will restore to you the years that the locust hath eaten, the cankerworm, and the caterpiller, and the palmerworm, my great army which I sent among you. And ye shall eat in plenty, and be satisfied, and praise the name of the Lord your God, that hath dealt wondrously with you, and my people shall never be ashamed. And ye shall know that I am in the midst of Israel, and that I am the Lord your God, and none else, and my people shall never be ashamed.

Notice the specific language of this promise: I will restore to you the years that the locust hath eaten. This is not a vague promise of general future blessing. It is a promise of restoration measured against a specific, identifiable loss, years, plural, consumed by an identifiable destroyer, the locust, the cankerworm, the caterpillar, the palmerworm. Whatever has eaten away at your life, your finances, your health, your relationships, your ministry, over however many years, God is not simply promising a fresh start disconnected from that loss. He is promising to restore, specifically, what was taken during those exact years. This is the heart of what this ministry means when it teaches believers to call their virtues back in the name of Jesus. We are not asking for generic blessing. We are asking for the specific years, the specific opportunities, the specific relationships and resources that the enemy has consumed, to be restored, not merely replaced with something different, but restored, returned, given back.

Job's story gives us the clearest narrative example of this kind of restoration actually taking place. After enduring catastrophic loss, the loss of his children, his wealth, his health, and nearly his marriage and his closest friendships, Job's account closes with words that have brought hope to suffering believers for thousands of years: and the Lord turned the captivity of Job, when he prayed for his friends, also the Lord gave Job twice as much as he had before. I want you to notice the sequence carefully. Job's restoration did not come while he was still consumed with his own suffering, understandable as that suffering was. It came specifically when he prayed for his friends, the same friends who had, throughout the book, offered him poor counsel and unfair accusations during his affliction. Job's willingness to intercede for the very people who had added to his pain, rather than remaining consumed with resentment toward them, appears directly connected to the timing of his restoration. This connects directly back to our chapter on forgiveness. Restoration and forgiveness are not separate, unrelated topics in Scripture. They are frequently intertwined, with genuine forgiveness often preceding and even releasing the restoration a believer has been seeking.

And the restoration itself was not merely equal to what Job had lost. It was doubled. The Lord gave Job twice as much as he had before. This is a pattern worth holding onto as you pray through the declarations at the end of this chapter: God's restoration is rarely a mere replacement of what was lost. It often exceeds it. This should shape our expectation as we call back what has been stolen from us. We are not simply asking for the return of exactly what we had before, though even that would be a tremendous mercy. We are standing on a biblical pattern where restoration frequently comes with increase attached to it.

This is precisely the spirit behind the recovery prayers this ministry has consistently prayed over believers pursuing restored virtue: any virtue, glory, or anointing that has left us through sex, food, gifts, friendship, or dreams, we command it back in the name of Jesus. Every witch, wizard, or spiritual source using our virtue, we declare that what belongs to us must be returned, and returned in full measure. Like the woman with the issue of blood, by faith we reach out and touch the hem of His garment and receive virtue from the Master. Every demonic transaction over our lives, every gift, every meal, every covenant that stole from us, we reverse it by the blood of Jesus.

I want to underscore something important here about the nature of these declarations. They are not incantations that manipulate spiritual forces through the correct combination of words. They are faith-filled, Scripture-rooted proclamations that align our mouths with what God has already promised and already accomplished through Jesus Christ. When we declare, I call my virtues back in the name of Jesus, we are exercising the same kind of active faith the woman with the issue of blood exercised when she reasoned within herself, if I may touch but his clothes, I shall be whole, and then acted immediately on that reasoning. Faith is never merely passive belief. It moves, it reaches, it speaks, it declares.

I also want to address directly a common question that arises in this part of the teaching: does calling back stolen virtue mean God was somehow prevented from blessing us until we spoke the right words? No. God's willingness to restore has never been in question. What is often in question is our own posture, whether we have positioned ourselves, through praise, thanksgiving, confession, forgiveness, and the invitation of the Holy Spirit, to receive what God has always been willing to give. The declarations of recall in this chapter are not attempts to convince a reluctant God. They are the confident, authoritative response of a believer who has done the necessary preparatory work described in the earlier chapters of this book, and who now stands ready to receive, actively and vocally, what God has already purposed to restore.

As you speak the declarations at the close of this chapter, I encourage you to name specifically, in your own words, what you believe has been taken from you across the years. Do not remain vague. The more specific your recall, the more specific your faith becomes, and the more specific your testimony will be once restoration comes. Just as Joel's promise named specific years and specific destroyers, name your own specific losses, and then declare, with full confidence, that they are coming back, and that in keeping with the pattern of Job, they may well come back doubled.

We should also examine the account of the prodigal son, because it offers us the clearest picture in all of Jesus' teaching of what restoration looks like from the perspective of a waiting, watching father. The younger son in this parable had squandered his entire inheritance in riotous living, had descended to feeding pigs in a foreign land, and had rehearsed a speech of confession he intended to deliver upon his return, hoping only to be received as a hired servant rather than a son. But Scripture tells us that while he was yet a great way off, his father saw him, and had compassion, and ran, and fell on his neck, and kissed him. The father did not wait passively for the son to arrive and grovel appropriately. He ran to meet him. And rather than assigning him a servant's role, the father called for the best robe, a ring for his hand, shoes for his feet, and a celebratory feast, saying, for this my son was dead, and is alive again, he was lost, and is found. This parable should shape our entire expectation of what recall and restoration look like in the heart of God. He does not restore us reluctantly, grudgingly, or with lingering suspicion about whether we deserve it. He runs toward us, robes us, and celebrates our return with a joy that exceeds our own capacity to fully receive it.

This is why the declarations at the close of this chapter are not spoken timidly or apologetically. They are spoken the way a son or daughter secure in their father's love speaks, with confidence rooted not in personal merit but in the character of the One who runs to meet returning children while they are yet a great way off. If you have felt hesitant to boldly call back what was taken from you, wondering whether you have somehow disqualified yourself through past failure, let the parable of the prodigal son settle that hesitation permanently. The father was already watching for his son's return. God is already watching for the return of everything the enemy has stolen from your life, and He is more eager to restore it to you than you are to receive it.

Chapter 11 — Healing and Wholeness

"Every fountain of affliction, plague, sickness, delay, and stagnation, dry up now, as at the touch of the Master."

Teaching

Restoration, as this book has described it through eleven chapters, is not complete until it reaches the body, because the Gospel has never been a message concerned only with the state of the soul while leaving the body untouched. From the earliest pages of the Old Testament through the healing ministry of Jesus and into the ongoing practice of the church, God has consistently revealed Himself as a healer, not merely of spiritual conditions, but of physical affliction, disease, and plague. This chapter draws that healing directly into our study of restored virtue, because the same virtue that flowed from Jesus to the woman with the issue of blood was, first and foremost, physical healing, the drying up of a fountain of blood that had drained her body for twelve years.

Isaiah's prophecy concerning the suffering servant gives us the clearest Old Testament foundation for understanding the connection between the cross and physical healing. Surely he hath borne our griefs, and carried our sorrows, yet we did esteem him stricken, smitten of God, and afflicted. But he was wounded for our transgressions, he was bruised for our iniquities, the chastisement of our peace was upon him, and with his stripes we are healed. This passage does something remarkable: it places physical healing, described here through the imagery of stripes, the physical wounds inflicted on Jesus during His scourging, directly alongside spiritual redemption from transgression and iniquity. The same suffering that accomplished our forgiveness also accomplished, according to this prophecy, our healing.

Peter, writing under the inspiration of the Holy Spirit centuries later, applies this same prophecy explicitly to believers living under the New Covenant: who his own self bare our sins in his own body on the tree, that we, being dead to sins, should live unto righteousness, by whose stripes ye were healed. Peter's grammar here is significant. He writes in the past tense, by whose stripes ye were healed, describing healing as an accomplished reality secured at the cross, not merely a future hope believers wait passively to receive. This does not mean every believer will never experience sickness in this present life, nor does it mean healing always manifests immediately or in the exact timing we would prefer. What it does mean is that healing, like forgiveness, has already been legally and spiritually secured through the finished work of Christ, and believers are invited to stand on that finished work with the same confidence the woman with the issue of blood stood on when she reached for the hem of Jesus' garment.

Let us return, once more, to that account, because Luke's Gospel records the final word of Jesus over her in a way that ties directly into this chapter's theme: and he said unto her, Daughter, be of good comfort, thy faith hath made thee whole, go in peace. The word translated whole here carries a meaning broader than mere physical cure. It encompasses wholeness, completeness, a restoration that touches every dimension of a person's life, physical, social, relational, and spiritual. She did not merely stop bleeding. She was made whole, restored fully into the community and family of God from which her illness had excluded her for twelve years.

This is the vision of healing this ministry holds to consistently, and it is the vision that shapes the specific prayers prayed over believers pursuing restored virtue: every fountain of affliction, plague, sickness, delay, and stagnation, dry up now, as at the touch of the Master. This prayer deliberately echoes the language of Mark's account, straightway the fountain of her blood was dried up, applying that exact pattern of instantaneous, complete healing to whatever fountain of affliction a believer is currently experiencing, whether a literal physical fountain of blood or a metaphorical fountain of financial drain, relational conflict, or spiritual stagnation that has continued for years without resolution.

We must be honest, in the spirit of this entire book's commitment to biblical faithfulness over sensationalism, that not every prayer for healing results in immediate, visible physical cure in this present life. Scripture itself records faithful believers, including some of Paul's own companions, who experienced illness that was not immediately resolved. This tension does not diminish the truth of Isaiah 53 and 1 Peter 2:24. It simply reminds us that we live between the already and the not yet, the healing already secured at the cross and the fullness of that healing that will be completely realized when Christ returns and every believer receives a glorified, incorruptible body. In the meantime, we are invited to pray boldly, to expect confidently, and to trust completely in the character of a God who has proven, throughout Scripture and throughout the testimony of countless believers across two thousand years, that He is still in the business of healing.

This chapter's connection to the broader theme of restored virtue is direct and important. Physical health is itself a form of virtue, an ability, a strength, a vitality that allows a believer to fulfil their calling, serve their family, and minister to others. When sickness has drained that strength over months or years, the restoration this book has been describing throughout must include the restoration of physical wholeness as well. This is why Psalm 103, our anchor text from the opening chapters of this book, places healing directly alongside forgiveness in its list of God's benefits: who forgiveth all thine iniquities, who healeth all thy diseases. These two are named together, in the same breath, by the same psalmist, describing the same God, because in the mind of Scripture, forgiveness and healing are companion mercies, both flowing from the same generous, covenant-keeping character of God.

As you close this chapter, I want to invite you to name specifically any area of physical, emotional, or relational affliction you are currently carrying, whatever fountain has continued draining you, whether for twelve years or twelve days. Bring it, as the woman did, into the crowd of your circumstances, and reach, by faith, for the hem of His garment. Whatever the outcome's timing, you can be certain of this: the same Jesus who felt virtue leave Him to heal her is not diminished, not depleted, not less willing today, to release that same virtue toward you.

James, writing to the scattered church, gives us practical instruction for how believers are meant to pursue healing corporately rather than only in private, individual faith: is any sick among you? let him call for the elders of the church, and let them pray over him, anointing him with oil in the name of the Lord, and the prayer of faith shall save the sick, and the Lord shall raise him up, and if he have committed sins, they shall be forgiven him. Notice, once again, the connection between physical healing and forgiveness of sin, appearing together in this passage just as they appeared together in Psalm 103. Notice also the specific instruction to call for the elders, rather than to suffer in isolated, private struggle. Healing, in the pattern Scripture consistently describes, is often released more fully within the context of gathered, praying community than in solitary struggle. This is one more reason this book has repeatedly emphasized fellowship, alongside Clubhouse gatherings and Saturday deliverance services, as an essential part of the restoration journey this teaching describes.

We should also address, briefly and honestly, the relationship between spiritual faith and appropriate medical care, since this teaching has never asked believers to reject wise medical counsel in favour of faith alone. The woman with the issue of blood had exhausted every available physician before she reached for Jesus, and her account is not meant to teach that physicians themselves are somehow opposed to faith, but rather that when human remedies have reached their limit, faith in Christ remains available and effective. This ministry consistently teaches believers to pursue qualified medical care alongside fervent prayer, understanding that God can and does work through skilled physicians, through medicine, and through medical technology, even as He also works through direct, supernatural intervention that bypasses natural means entirely. Wisdom, not either-or thinking, should guide every believer's pursuit of healing.

Chapter 12 — Sealing and Walking in Restored Virtue

"I seal every prayer, declaration, and impartation with the blood of Jesus. Let the blood cover my life, my family, my finances, my ministry, my movements, and my dreams."

Teaching

We have travelled a long road together through this book, from understanding what virtue is, through the sobering study of how it is lost, into the deep well of the woman with the issue of blood, through praise and thanksgiving, confession and forgiveness, the invitation of the Holy Ghost, the breaking of every yoke, the active recall of what was stolen, and the pursuit of healing and wholeness. This final chapter addresses a step that must never be skipped, because everything gained through the previous eleven chapters can be placed at risk if this final step is neglected: sealing.

Sealing, in the biblical sense this ministry consistently teaches, is the act of securing, protecting, and confirming what God has restored, so that it cannot be easily undone, stolen back, or eroded by the same enemy who took it in the first place. Paul's letter to the Ephesians gives us the theological foundation for understanding what it means to be sealed: in whom ye also trusted, after that ye heard the word of truth, the gospel of your salvation, in whom also after that ye believed, ye were sealed with that holy Spirit of promise. Notice the sequence Paul describes: hearing the word of truth, believing it, and then being sealed. This sealing is not something believers accomplish through their own effort or vigilance. It is the Holy Spirit Himself who seals a believer, functioning, as the rest of Ephesians makes clear, as the earnest, the down payment, the guarantee of the fuller inheritance still to come.

This has direct application to everything this book has walked you through. The restoration you have pursued through these chapters, the recalled virtue, the broken yokes, the healed body, the forgiven and forgiving heart, must now be sealed by the same Holy Spirit who accomplished it in the first place. This is not a matter of anxious self-protection, constantly worried the enemy will steal back what was restored. It is a confident, faith-filled act of trusting the Holy Spirit's ongoing, sealing presence to secure what He has already worked to restore.

The writer of Hebrews gives us the second crucial anchor for this chapter's teaching, pointing us to the ultimate basis of our confidence: and to Jesus the mediator of the new covenant, and to the blood of sprinkling, that speaketh better things than that of Abel. This verse draws a deliberate contrast between the blood of Abel, which cried out from the ground for vengeance after Cain murdered his brother, and the blood of Jesus, which speaks a better word, not vengeance but mercy, not condemnation but covenant, not accusation but access into the very presence of God. Every declaration, every prayer, every act of restoration described throughout this book has been made possible by this sprinkled blood, and it is this same blood that seals everything we have prayed and declared throughout these chapters.

This is precisely why this ministry closes every deliverance service, and why we close this book, with a deliberate sealing prayer, rooted entirely in the blood of Jesus: I seal every prayer, declaration, and impartation with the blood of Jesus. Let the blood cover my life, my family, my finances, my ministry, my movements, and my dreams. Every demon assigned to reverse this altar, be scattered by the blood of Jesus. This is not superstition or magical thinking about blood as a substance. It is a theological confession that the same blood which cleansed us in our chapter on confession, the same blood which washed us from our sins and made us kings and priests unto God, is the blood that now seals, protects, and secures everything that has been restored to us through this teaching.

I want to give you nine practical, prophetic directions as we close this book, directions drawn directly from the pattern of ongoing discipleship this ministry has consistently taught, so that the restoration you have received through these twelve chapters becomes a sustained, lasting reality rather than a temporary spiritual high that fades within days of finishing this book. Consider committing to a season of fasting, following the pattern of consecrated, focused prayer and fasting this ministry regularly leads its members through, understanding fasting not as a ritual that earns God's favour, but as a discipline that sharpens spiritual sensitivity and demonstrates earnest hunger for what God has promised. Use anointing oil, when you have it available, as a simple point of contact for your faith, prayed over and applied with expectancy, always understanding the oil itself carries no power apart from the faith it represents. Guard your senses, remembering our earlier study of Proverbs 23:6, discerning carefully what you receive, from whom, and under what circumstances, especially regarding food, gifts, intimacy, and counsel. Practice almsgiving with discernment, blessing those in genuine need with a pure heart, while remaining alert to any gift or transaction offered with a hidden, exploitative intention. Continue tithing and giving as an act of worship and trust, not out of compulsion or fear, remembering that the blessing of the Lord makes rich and adds no sorrow with it. Stay immersed in the Word daily, particularly the passages this book has centred around, Psalm 103, Psalm 51, Psalm 119:18, Mark 5:25 through 34, and Luke 8:43 through 48, allowing faith to continue growing through ongoing hearing of these Scriptures. Remain in fellowship with a gathered body of believers, understanding that restoration is meant to be lived out in community, not merely in private devotion. Continue in prayer without ceasing, carrying the rhythm of praise, thanksgiving, confession, and declaration you have practiced throughout this book into your ongoing daily life. And finally, watch for the ongoing evidence of restored virtue in your life, and when you see it, testify, publicly and unashamedly, the way the woman with the issue of blood testified before the whole crowd, because your testimony becomes someone else's invitation to believe.

As we close this final chapter and this book, I want to speak a benediction over you, the reader, wherever and whenever you are reading these words. May the same virtue that flowed from Jesus to the woman with the issue of blood flow freely into every drained, weary, and waiting place in your life. May every year the locust has eaten be restored to you, and may it be restored with increase, according to the pattern of Job. May every yoke be broken because of the anointing, and may you walk, from this day forward, not merely free from oppression, but established in the dominion Christ has already purchased for you. May the Holy Ghost fill you the way He filled the house at Pentecost, may His fire refine you, and may His seal secure everything that has been restored to you through this teaching. And may you go from these pages the way the woman went from that crowd two thousand years ago, in peace, made whole, carrying a testimony the enemy can never again take from you.

Breaking yokes. Restoring destinies. Establishing dominion. This is the work Christ has already begun in you. Walk in it, in the mighty and everlasting name of Jesus.

Consider, finally, the account of the twelve baskets gathered after Jesus fed the five thousand with five loaves and two fishes. After everyone had eaten and were filled, Jesus instructed His disciples, gather up the fragments that remain, that nothing be lost, and they gathered them, and filled twelve baskets with the fragments. This detail is easy to overlook, but it carries real significance for our closing chapter. Jesus did not consider the miracle complete simply because the crowd had been fed. He insisted on the deliberate, careful gathering and preservation of what remained, ensuring nothing of what had been provided was carelessly wasted or lost. This is the spirit of sealing this final chapter calls you toward. Having received restoration through the previous eleven chapters, having called back stolen virtue, broken every yoke, and received healing for your body and your heart, do not now allow carelessness, complacency, or a return to old patterns to waste what God has so generously restored. Gather the fragments. Guard what has been given. Let nothing be lost.

I want to leave you with one final image as we close this book together. Think again of the woman with the issue of blood, walking away from that crowded street, no longer marked by twelve years of affliction, no longer isolated from the community of faith, but called daughter, sent in peace, made whole. She did not return to her old identity. She did not quietly slip back into the shadows of her former condition. She carried, from that day forward, a testimony and an identity entirely reshaped by her encounter with Jesus. This is who you are being invited to become as you close this book: not the same person who opened its first page, still wondering what happened to the virtue that once flowed so freely, but a son or daughter who has pressed through the crowd, reached for the hem of His garment, and received, fully and completely, everything God intended to restore.
